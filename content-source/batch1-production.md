## PAGE 1 — Daily Puppy Schedule

**E. Primary Search Intent:** "What should my puppy's whole day look like?"

**F. Article**

---

Puppies do best on a repeating loop, not a single fixed clock. The loop is: **wake → potty → eat or play → short activity → nap → repeat.** Below is a sample version of that loop, followed by how to build your own and how it shifts as your puppy grows.

### Sample Daily Puppy Schedule

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 6:30 AM | Wake up, straight outside to potty |
| 6:45 AM | Breakfast |
| 7:00 AM | Potty again |
| 7:15 AM | Play or short training (5–10 min) |
| 7:45 AM | Nap |
| 9:30 AM | Wake, potty, short play |
| 10:00 AM | Nap |
| 12:00 PM | Wake, potty, lunch (if on 3 meals), short training |
| 12:45 PM | Nap |
| 2:30 PM | Wake, potty, play |
| 3:15 PM | Nap |
| 5:00 PM | Wake, potty, dinner |
| 5:30 PM | Family time, gentle play |
| 7:00 PM | Potty, calm wind-down (chew toy, low activity) |
| 8:30 PM | Final potty break |
| 9:00 PM | Bedtime |

Treat the clock times as a shape, not a mandate — the pattern (wake, potty, activity, rest, repeat) is what matters, not hitting 6:30 AM exactly.

### Why the Loop Matters

A very young puppy spends most of the day asleep. Around two months old, a puppy's actual *awake* time typically adds up to only a few hours spread across the whole day — the rest is sleep.<sup>1</sup> That's why the schedule above has so many naps: it's not overscheduling, it's matching a puppy's real capacity. A puppy that seems suddenly cranky or bitey in the evening is very often simply overtired, not badly behaved.

The potty timing in the loop follows the same logic as crate-time planning: after a puppy has just emptied its bladder, general veterinary guidance suggests roughly one hour of control per month of age at that specific moment — a 2-month-old might manage about 3 hours, a 3-month-old about 4, before needing another break.<sup>2</sup> That number shrinks fast around excitement, drinking, or waking up, so treat it as a planning ceiling, not a guarantee.

### How the Schedule Changes by Age

- **8–10 weeks:** Very frequent everything — potty breaks, naps, tiny training bursts. Expect to be "on shift" most of the day.
- **10–12 weeks:** Slightly longer stretches between potty breaks; short structured training sessions become worth adding.
- **12+ weeks:** Fewer, longer naps; more consistent stretches awake; socialization outings (see below) become a bigger part of the day.

*(Full breakdowns: [8-Week-Old Puppy Schedule], [10-Week-Old Puppy Schedule], [12-Week-Old Puppy Schedule] — links to be activated when those pages publish.)*

### What Belongs in the Day Beyond Potty and Meals

- **Short training bursts** (5–10 minutes, several times a day) beat one long session — puppies this age have short attention spans.
- **Socialization** — safe, calm exposure to new people, sounds, surfaces, and (appropriately vaccinated, healthy) dogs is time-sensitive; the primary window is generally considered to be the first three months of life.<sup>3</sup>
- **Rest** — genuinely enforced nap time, not just "quiet room" — is part of training, not a break from it.
- **Wind-down** before bed — lower the energy in the house 30–60 minutes before the final potty break, rather than going from full play straight to the crate.

### Building Your Own Schedule

1. Write down your actual wake time and the times you're realistically home.
2. Block in potty breaks first — after waking, after eating, after play, and roughly every couple of hours in between for a very young puppy.
3. Fit meals around those breaks (most puppies do best on 3–4 meals a day at 8–12 weeks).
4. Add short training and play blocks after meals or naps, when your puppy is alert.
5. Protect nap time — a puppy who's kept awake too long tends to get wound up and mouthy, not tired.

Every puppy's real day is shaped by *your* wake time, work schedule, and puppy's actual age — a single static table can only get you so close. **[This is the natural bridge to the Personalized Puppy Plan tool, which is not yet live: describe it as "coming soon" rather than implying it's active.]**

## PAGE 2 — Puppy Potty Training (Hub)

**A. URL:** /potty-training/
**B. Recommended H1:** How to Potty Train a Puppy: Complete Step-by-Step Guide
**C. SEO Title:** How to Potty Train a Puppy: The Complete System | Dog Plan Lab
**D. Meta Description:** A step-by-step puppy potty training system — routine, supervision, signal recognition, and a troubleshooting table for common accidents.
**E. Primary Search Intent:** "How do I potty train my puppy?"

**F. Article**

---

Potty training a puppy comes down to four things done consistently: a predictable routine, constant supervision or confinement when you can't supervise, quickly rewarding success outside, and managing accidents without punishment. There's no shortcut around consistency, but there is a system that makes it faster.

### The Puppy Potty Training System

1. **Set a routine.** Take your puppy out first thing in the morning, after every meal, after every nap, after play, and roughly every 1–2 hours in between for a very young puppy. (See the [Potty Training Schedule] for age-specific timing.)
2. **Supervise or confine — always one or the other.** When you can't watch your puppy directly, use a crate, playpen, or leash-in-hand so accidents can't happen unnoticed. This is the single biggest lever in how fast potty training goes.
3. **Go to the same spot outside** every time, and **stay out long enough** — often 5–10 minutes, without distraction — rather than coming back in the second your puppy finishes.
4. **Reward immediately after elimination**, while still outside — not once you're back at the door.
5. **Watch for the signals**: circling, sniffing intently, sudden stillness, heading toward the door. Puppies who are supervised closely start giving these cues before an accident, not after.
6. **Handle accidents without punishment.** Interrupt calmly if you catch it happening, take the puppy outside, and clean the indoor spot thoroughly. Punishing after the fact teaches fear, not house training — a puppy can't connect a delayed correction to the act.

### Nighttime Potty Training

Many puppies gain the ability to get through the night without a potty break by around 3 months old, though this varies by individual puppy.<sup>1</sup> Until then, expect at least one overnight trip outside, kept low-key: minimal lights, no play, straight back to bed afterward so your puppy doesn't learn that nighttime waking means playtime.

### How Long Potty Training Realistically Takes

There's no universal number — it depends on the puppy, consistency, and how much supervision is realistic in your household. What's true across the board: progress is rarely a straight line. A puppy who's been reliable for two weeks can still have a setback (see "Regression" below) — that's normal, not a sign the training failed.

### Troubleshooting Table

| Problem | Likely Reason | What to Do Next |
|---|---|---|
| Pees indoors shortly after coming back in from outside | Cut the outside trip short, or was distracted and didn't fully empty | Stay outside longer (5–10 min), minimize distractions, wait for a second elimination before heading in — see [Puppy Pees Inside After Going Outside] |
| Won't go outside at all, then goes indoors | Surface preference, fear, or overstimulation outside | Try a calmer, more boring potty spot; rule out fear of the environment — see [Puppy Won't Pee Outside] |
| Sudden accidents after weeks of success | Regression is common; can also be schedule change, new environment, or a medical cause | Tighten supervision back up temporarily; if frequent, straining, or accompanied by other symptoms, check with a vet |
| Accidents specifically in the crate | Crate too large, confinement period too long for current bladder capacity, or anxiety | Check crate sizing, shorten confinement windows — see [Crate Training Guide] |
| Frequent urination, straining, or blood in urine | Possible medical issue (e.g., urinary tract infection) rather than a training problem | Stop treating as a training issue — schedule a veterinary visit |

### When to Involve a Vet

Frequent accidents that don't respond to tightened supervision, straining to urinate, blood in urine, or a sudden change in a previously reliable puppy's habits are reasons to rule out a medical cause before assuming it's a training gap.

## PAGE 3 — Puppy Biting (Hub)

**A. URL:** /puppy-biting/
**B. Recommended H1:** Puppy Biting: Why Puppies Bite and How to Stop It
**C. SEO Title:** Puppy Biting: Why It Happens and How to Stop It | Dog Plan Lab
**D. Meta Description:** Why puppies bite, what to do the moment teeth touch skin, and a do-this-not-that framework for stopping it without punishment.
**E. Primary Search Intent:** "My puppy keeps biting me — what do I do?"

**F. Article**

---

Puppy biting is normal. It's mostly a form of social play, not primarily a teething behavior — teething shows up more as gnawing on objects than as biting people.<sup>1</sup> That doesn't make it pleasant to live with, but it does mean you're not dealing with a "bad" puppy — you're teaching a skill called bite inhibition: learning to control how hard the mouth closes.

### The 60-Second Biting Response

The moment teeth touch skin or clothing:

1. **Stop moving and go still or make a short "ow" sound** — a startle response, not anger.
2. **Withdraw attention for about 10–20 seconds** — stand up, turn away, or briefly leave the immediate area.
3. **Redirect to an appropriate toy** once things are calm — offer it before re-engaging, not as a chase.
4. **Resume calmly.** If the puppy mouths gently, that's success — resume play. If teeth come out hard again, repeat the withdrawal.

This mirrors what puppies do with each other: a littermate that gets bitten too hard yelps and stops playing, which is the feedback that teaches bite pressure control.<sup>2</sup> You're standing in for that feedback loop.

### Why Puppies Bite

- **Play and exploration** — the primary driver at this age, especially during excitement.
- **Overtiredness/overstimulation** — biting spikes are common in the evening when a puppy has been awake too long (see [Puppy Biting at Night]).
- **Teething discomfort** — more associated with wanting to chew on objects; offering appropriate chew toys addresses this specifically.
- **Attention-seeking** — if biting reliably gets a reaction (even a negative one), it can become a way to start interaction.

### DO THIS / NOT THIS

| Situation | Do This | Not This |
|---|---|---|
| Teeth touch your hand during play | Go still, withdraw attention briefly, redirect to a toy | Pull your hand away quickly (can trigger chase) or yell |
| Puppy is grabbing at pant legs while walking | Stop moving, redirect with a toy, resume once calm | Keep walking (turns your clothing into a tug toy) or kick/push the puppy away |
| Puppy bites harder than expected | Yelp or say "ow," pause the interaction | Hit, scruff, or hold the muzzle shut |
| Puppy is overtired and bitey in the evening | End stimulation, start a calm wind-down routine | Keep playing to "tire them out" (often backfires) |
| Puppy won't stop after redirecting repeatedly | End the play session and give a calm-down break | Continue engaging through escalating biting |

### Mistakes That Make Biting Worse

- Physical corrections (hitting, scruffing, holding the mouth shut) — not recommended; these can create fear or, in some puppies, escalate the behavior rather than reduce it.
- Rough hand-wrestling games, especially early on — they can teach that hands are appropriate biting targets.
- Continuing to engage once a puppy is clearly overstimulated, hoping it will "wear itself out."

### Children and Biting

Supervise interactions between puppies and young children closely. A puppy that's fine one-on-one with an adult can get more aroused and mouthier around a running, high-pitched child. Teach kids to go still and quiet (not scream or run) if a puppy starts mouthing, and keep initial interactions short and supervised.

### When Biting Needs Professional Attention

Occasional hard nips during normal puppy play are expected. If biting hasn't meaningfully softened by around 6 months of age despite consistent, calm redirection, that's a reasonable point to bring in an experienced trainer or behavior professional rather than continuing to wait it out.<sup>3</sup> Biting that involves genuine aggression signals — stiff body language, growling with a hard stare, guarding — rather than loose, bouncy play, warrants professional input sooner.

## PAGE 4 — Puppy Crate Training (Hub)

**A. URL:** /crate-training/
**B. Recommended H1:** How to Crate Train a Puppy: Step-by-Step Guide
**C. SEO Title:** How to Crate Train a Puppy: Step-by-Step Guide | Dog Plan Lab
**D. Meta Description:** A humane, gradual crate training method for puppies — sizing, the first 3 days, a troubleshooting table, and when crying signals something more.
**E. Primary Search Intent:** "How do I crate train my puppy?"

**F. Article**

---

Done correctly, a crate becomes a puppy's own safe space, not a punishment. Dogs generally avoid eliminating where they sleep, which is part of why crate training is considered one of the fastest, most effective house-training tools when it's introduced gradually and positively.<sup>1</sup> The key word is *gradually* — rushing the process is the most common reason crate training goes badly.

### Choosing a Crate

Pick a crate large enough for your puppy to stand, turn around, and lie down comfortably — not much bigger. An oversized crate lets a puppy use one end as a bathroom and the other as a bed, which works against house training. If you're sizing for an adult dog, use a divider panel to shrink the usable space while your puppy is small.

### The First 3 Days of Crate Training

**Day 1 — Make the crate a good place to be, door open.**
Toss treats inside throughout the day. Feed one full meal inside with the door open. Let your puppy wander in and out freely — no pressure, no closing the door yet.

**Day 2 — Very short closures, with you right there.**
Once your puppy is entering the crate on its own for food, close the door for a few seconds while you sit nearby, then open it before any fussing starts. Repeat several short sessions through the day, gradually stretching to a few minutes.

**Day 3 — Build duration and add a short absence.**
Extend closed-door time to 10–20 minutes with a stuffed chew toy, staying in the room but not interacting. Try one short session where you leave the room for a minute or two and come back before your puppy gets upset.

If at any point your puppy shows real distress — excessive barking, panting, cowering — that's a signal to back up a step, not push through.<sup>2</sup> This process commonly takes longer than 3 days for many puppies; treat this as a starting structure, not a deadline.

### Building Toward Full Duration

After the first few days, extend gradually — a matter of days to weeks, not a fixed timeline. As a planning guide, a puppy can generally manage more time in the crate after just having eliminated than right after waking or drinking, and daytime confinement generally shouldn't exceed about 2–3 hours for a young puppy who can't yet hold it longer.<sup>3</sup> Overnight is different: many puppies can get through the night without needing a potty break by around 3 months old, even though they couldn't manage that duration during an active day.<sup>4</sup>

### Nighttime Crate Training

Keep the crate near you at first (many owners start it in or near the bedroom) so you can hear if your puppy genuinely needs a potty trip versus just settling in. If crying starts, pause before responding — don't let a puppy out of the crate the instant it fusses, or the fussing gets reinforced; wait for a brief lull, then let them out calmly.<sup>5</sup>

### Troubleshooting Table

| Problem | Likely Reason | What to Do Next |
|---|---|---|
| Cries every time the door closes, even briefly | Duration increased too fast | Go back to shorter, door-open sessions; rebuild slowly |
| Won't enter the crate at all | Negative association forming, or moved too fast | Return to Day 1 (open door, treats only); never force entry |
| Fine during the day, cries hard specifically at night | May be a genuine potty need, or just settling-in protest — hard to tell apart at first | Rule out potty need first; if fed/pottied and still distressed, treat as settling and avoid instantly releasing |
| Accidents happen in the crate | Crate too large, or confinement period longer than current bladder capacity | Reduce crate size with a divider; shorten time between breaks |
| Ongoing distress, destruction, or vocalization that doesn't improve with a slower approach | May indicate separation anxiety rather than typical adjustment | Behaviorist input may be needed — this goes beyond standard crate training troubleshooting<sup>6</sup> |

### Common Mistakes

- Moving too fast through the steps because the puppy "seemed fine" on day one.
- Using the crate as punishment — this undoes the safe-space association fast.
- Leaving a puppy in the crate far longer than its current bladder capacity, then being confused about accidents.
- Letting a puppy out the instant it whines, every time — teaches that whining works.

### When Distress Signals You Need a Different Approach

If a puppy consistently panics in the crate despite a slow, positive introduction — not just protesting briefly, but genuine sustained distress — some puppies do better in an alternative confinement setup (a playpen or puppy-proofed room) while separation-related concerns are addressed, ideally with a veterinarian or qualified behavior professional involved.

## PAGE 5 — First Week With a New Puppy

**A. URL:** /new-puppy/first-week/
**B. Recommended H1:** Your Puppy's First Week Home: A Simple Day-by-Day Plan
**C. SEO Title:** Puppy's First Week Home: Day-by-Day Plan | Dog Plan Lab
**D. Meta Description:** What actually matters during your puppy's first week home — a simple before/day 1, first night, days 2–7 plan, without a 47-item to-do list.
**E. Primary Search Intent:** "I just brought home a puppy — what am I supposed to do this first week?"

**F. Article**

---

You don't need to get everything right this week. You need to get a small number of things right: safety, a predictable rhythm, and calm first nights. Everything else can wait.

### First Week Priority Checklist

Before anything else, this week is really about:

1. Keeping your puppy safe (puppy-proofing, supervision or confinement at all times)
2. Starting a loose daily rhythm (wake, potty, eat, nap, repeat — see [Daily Puppy Schedule])
3. Getting through the first few nights without a crisis
4. Beginning gentle house training from day one
5. Low-key, positive early exposure to normal household life

That's the list. Not socialization classes, not perfect commands, not a fully trained dog by Sunday.

### Before / Day 1

- Puppy-proof one main area: remove chewable hazards, block off inaccessible rooms, secure cords.
- Set up the crate or confinement area before your puppy arrives, not after the first accident.
- Decide on the potty spot outside and plan to use it consistently from the first trip.
- Keep Day 1 low-key. A quiet arrival with minimal visitors beats a big welcome party — puppies are absorbing an enormous amount of new information already.
- First potty trip: as soon as you arrive, before anything else.

### The First Night

The first night is often the hardest part of the week, not because anything's wrong, but because your puppy has never slept away from its mother and littermates before. Some crying the first night or two is common and doesn't necessarily mean something is wrong — it's a big adjustment, not a crisis.<sup>1</sup>

- Keep the crate or sleeping area near you, at least for the first stretch, so you can hear if a potty trip is actually needed.
- Plan for at least one overnight potty break — keep it boring (dim light, no play, straight back to bed).
- If there's crying that isn't a potty need, avoid springing into an entertaining response — a calm, brief presence is different from starting a play session at 2 AM.
- Don't expect (or aim for) a perfect night. A rough first night or two is common and not a sign you're doing something wrong.

### Days 2–3

- Settle into the rhythm from the Daily Puppy Schedule — same general shape each day, even if exact times shift.
- Begin gentle house training in earnest: consistent potty spot, reward immediately after elimination outside.
- Start very short, low-pressure handling — brief touches to paws, ears, mouth — paired with something pleasant, to build comfort for future grooming and vet visits.
- Let visitors be calm and infrequent; this isn't the week for a houseful of excited guests.

### Days 4–7

- Introduce very short training bursts (a few minutes) — name recognition, coming when called across a room, is plenty for week one.
- If your puppy is healthy and has had appropriate initial vaccination guidance from your vet, low-risk socialization can typically begin — carried people, calm car rides, controlled exposure to new sounds — since the primary socialization window is considered to be the first three months of life and many veterinary behaviorists support starting safe socialization before a puppy is fully vaccinated.<sup>2</sup> Confirm timing specifics with your own veterinarian, since it depends on your individual puppy's health and vaccination status.
- Start noticing your puppy's individual patterns — when it's genuinely tired vs. wound up, its typical potty timing, what settles it.

### End-of-Week Checkpoint

By the end of week one, a reasonable bar is:
- Your puppy has a loose, repeating daily rhythm.
- House training has started, even if accidents are still happening.
- Nights are survivable, even if not perfect.
- You've started noticing your puppy's individual signals.

If you're behind this list, that's normal too — every puppy and household adjusts at a different pace.
