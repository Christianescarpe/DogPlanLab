<AnswerFirstSummary>
  No — controlled research found winning or losing tug-of-war does not affect the "dominance" dimension of the dog-owner relationship; a more useful finding is that who initiates play matters more than who wins.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## The Popular Worry, and What Research Actually Found

A study found dogs allowed to win repeated tug sessions showed no consistent difference in the dominance-related dimension of their relationship compared to dogs made to lose.<sup>1</sup> The popular caution isn't supported.

## The More Genuinely Useful Finding

A follow-up study found dogs that initiated most of their play sessions themselves scored lower on amenability and were more likely to show aggression-related behavior in the broader relationship assessment.<sup>2</sup> A real, specific reason to be the one who initiates and ends tug sessions.

## How to Play Tug Reasonably

1. You initiate and end sessions.
2. Teach a reliable "drop it"/trade cue (see existing [Teaching Drop It](/puppy-training/drop-it/)).
3. Winning or losing occasionally is fine.
4. Watch arousal (see [Puppy Play](/puppy-play/)) and end before it escalates.
5. Guarding behavior around the toy specifically is different — see existing [Aggression or Play](/puppy-biting/aggression-or-play/).

## Quick Reference

| Popular claim | What research shows |
|---|---|
| Letting your puppy win causes dominance/aggression | Not supported |
| Who initiates play matters | Supported |
| Tug is inherently dangerous/bad | Not supported |
| Toy guarding is a real, distinct concern | Yes |

## Common Mistakes

- Avoiding letting your puppy win out of an unsupported worry.
- Letting your puppy demand/initiate every session.
- Ignoring real guarding signals.
