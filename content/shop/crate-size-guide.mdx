<AnswerFirstSummary>
  Size for your puppy's expected adult size and use a divider panel to shrink the usable space during puppyhood — this is both more cost-effective (one crate instead of several) and behaviorally correct, since a crate that's too large during house training can let a puppy soil one end and sleep in the other.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## How to Choose

The crate should let your adult dog stand up, turn around, and lie down stretched out comfortably — not much more. Buying to adult size with a divider panel avoids repurchasing as your puppy grows, and is the approach most current crate manufacturers explicitly design for (MidWest, Frisco, and Diggs all sell dedicated divider panels or include one standard).

## What to Compare

Divider-panel availability and compatibility; interior dimensions vs. your puppy's projected adult size (use a breed weight/height estimate from your vet or breeder if unsure); tray/base depth (a puppy who's an occasional messy eater or has occasional accidents benefits from a raised-lip removable tray).

## Best For / Not Ideal For

A divider-panel approach is best for most puppies of a breed with a reasonably predictable adult size. If you have a mixed-breed puppy of very uncertain adult size, a mid-size crate with a plan to upsize once size becomes clearer may be more practical than guessing at the largest possible size upfront.

## Common Mistakes

- Buying "cute puppy-sized" instead of planning for adult size.
- Skipping the divider panel to save a few dollars, then fighting house-training setbacks it would have prevented.
- Assuming every crate model's divider is interchangeable across brands — they generally are not.

## Safety and Behavior Red Flags

A too-large undivided space during early house training can undermine the crate's den-instinct benefit (see existing [Crate Training](/crate-training/)); check that the divider panel itself has no sharp edges or pinch points a curious puppy could interact with.
