<AnswerFirstSummary>
  Ignoring works specifically for attention-reinforced barking, and only if genuinely complete and consistent — it's the wrong tool for fear-driven, pain-related, or alarm barking.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## Why "Just Ignore It" Isn't Universal

This advice only makes sense for barking maintained by getting a reaction. For fear, pain, or genuine alarm, ignoring doesn't address the driver at all.

## When Ignoring Is the Right Tool

Barking that reliably follows a request, no fear signals present, and you can genuinely withhold the reaction consistently.

## When Ignoring Is the Wrong Tool

Fear-driven barking (see [Puppy Fear](/puppy-fear/)); pain/distress (veterinary evaluation, not wait-and-see); genuine alarm (a real event, not demand barking).

## What "Should You Say No" Really Means

Scolding still counts as attention in most cases and can reinforce the behavior. Physical management (moving your puppy away from a trigger) is different from a verbal correction and can be appropriate even when "no" isn't useful.

## What to Expect if Ignoring Is Right Here

A temporary increase before decrease (extinction burst) is commonly documented. Giving in because of this increase makes the pattern more persistent.

## Decision Guide

| Situation | Ignoring appropriate? |
|---|---|
| Attention/demand, no fear signals, can be consistently withheld | Yes |
| Fear-driven | No — see [Puppy Fear](/puppy-fear/) |
| Possible pain/distress | No — veterinary evaluation first |
| Genuine alarm | No |
| Can't actually be consistent | Reconsider |

## Common Mistakes

- Applying "just ignore it" universally.
- Giving in because of a temporary increase.
- Using scolding as a substitute for physical management.

## Safety and Behavior Red Flags

Any possibility of pain, fear, or genuine distress — don't apply ignoring; address the actual cause.
