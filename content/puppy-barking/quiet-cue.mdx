<AnswerFirstSummary>
  Teach "quiet" by getting a brief pause (via redirection, not punishment), rewarding that pause, then attaching a cue word — rewarding the quiet moment specifically reinforces the quiet, not the bark that preceded it.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## Addressing a Common Worry First

Rewarding right after barking sounds risky, but if you reward the quiet moment specifically — after barking has stopped — you're reinforcing quiet, not the preceding bark. Timing matters.

## The Step-by-Step Method

1. In a low-stakes situation, get attention with a treat near the nose — sniffing/eating and barking don't happen simultaneously.
2. Mark the instant they go quiet, then reward.
3. Once reliable, add the word "quiet" right as the pause happens.
4. Gradually extend required quiet duration; fade the visible treat presentation.
5. Practice in low-stakes contexts before expecting it during a real trigger (see [Training Around Distractions](/puppy-training/distracted/)).

## Applying It in Real Situations

Use "quiet" alongside trigger-specific management from the other spokes — a tool within those approaches, not a replacement.

## Training Sequence Checklist

- [ ] Practiced the treat-interrupt step calmly
- [ ] Consistently rewarding the pause, not the bark
- [ ] Word "quiet" added only once the pause is reliable
- [ ] Duration gradually extended
- [ ] Practiced with mild distraction before a real trigger

## Common Mistakes

- Adding "quiet" too early.
- Expecting it to work during a high-intensity real trigger before easier practice.
- Shouting "quiet!" as a correction.

## Safety and Behavior Red Flags

No response to this approach despite consistent, correctly sequenced practice suggests the underlying cause isn't attention/demand-based — revisit [Why Is My Puppy Barking?](/puppy-barking/).
