<AnswerFirstSummary>
  There isn't a reliable universal day-by-day schedule — authoritative sources on crate training consistently describe progression as response-based, not calendar-based, and can take anywhere from days to several weeks depending on the puppy.<sup>1,2</sup> What actually determines your pace is how your puppy responds at each stage, not which day you're on. Below is a structure built around that reality — five stages, each with clear signs you're ready to move on, and clear signs you've moved too fast.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## The Progression, Stage by Stage

| Stage | What's Happening | Ready for the Next Stage When... |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Comfort entering the crate** | Door open, treats/meals inside, puppy enters freely | Puppy walks in calmly on its own, without being lured every time |
| **2. Door briefly closed** | Door closed for seconds at a time while puppy eats or chews, then reopened before any fussing | Puppy stays calm and settled with the door closed for short stretches, no escalating distress |
| **3. Calm with you nearby** | Door closed longer, you're in the room but not interacting | Puppy settles or naps with the door closed while you're present |
| **4. Brief movement or absence** | You leave the room briefly, then return | Puppy remains calm during a short absence, doesn't escalate when you're out of sight |
| **5. Real-world use** | Longer daytime naps, overnight, or actual owner-away stretches | Puppy handles the specific real-world use case you're building toward (see below) |

**This is a framework, not a countdown.** Some puppies move through Stages 1–3 in a couple of days; others need a week or more at a single stage. Both are normal. The stage you're on matters far more than the day count.

## How to Know You're Ready to Advance

- Your puppy settles or naps rather than staying alert/fidgety at the current stage.
- Brief vocalizing, pawing, or general fidgeting when newly confined at a stage is common and not itself a reason to stop — that's a normal initial reaction, not a signal to back up.<sup>3</sup>

## Signs You're Progressing Too Quickly

Genuine distress signs — not brief settling-in fuss — mean back up a stage rather than push forward: persistent salivating, ongoing vocalization that doesn't taper off, active escape attempts, or an inability to settle at all during a session.<sup>3</sup> If you see these, this is also a reasonable point to check in with your veterinarian before continuing the plan as-is, rather than assuming more repetition alone will fix it.<sup>3</sup>

## After a Setback

A setback (a session that goes badly, a stretch of regression) doesn't mean starting over from zero. Go back one stage — not all the way to Stage 1 — and spend more time there before trying to advance again. Rushing back to the stage where things went wrong, rather than easing into it, tends to recreate the same setback.

## Daytime vs. Nighttime Progression

These often move at different paces, and that's normal — a puppy can be comfortable with daytime naps in the crate well before (or after) handling overnight stretches well, and vice versa. Treat them as two separate tracks through the same stage framework rather than expecting one to automatically transfer to the other, alongside the rest of the [Daily Puppy Schedule](/puppy-schedule/). (See [Puppy Crying in the Crate](/crate-training/crying/) and [Puppy Crying in the Crate at Night](/crate-training/crying-at-night/).)

## Potty and Feeding Context

Always begin a crate session after a potty opportunity, not before — a puppy that needs to eliminate will struggle at any stage regardless of how well the training itself is going. (Full method: [Potty Training Guide](/potty-training/).) Feeding meals in the crate (door open, early on) is a legitimate way to build positive association at Stage 1, not just a separate feeding-logistics choice.

## Building Toward Owner-Away Stretches

For the specific goal of leaving your puppy crated while you're out of the house: extend Stage 4 gradually and specifically for this — brief real departures (not just leaving the room), building duration slowly, and ideally monitoring the first few actual departures (a camera works well) to confirm your puppy is settled rather than distressed while you're gone.<sup>3</sup> If your puppy shows signs of distress specifically tied to your departure (not just confinement) that don't improve with a slower approach, this is worth discussing with your veterinarian rather than continuing to push through it alone.<sup>4</sup>

## Ready for the Next Step? Checklist

- [ ] Puppy has settled or napped at the current stage more than once, not just as a one-time success
- [ ] No escalating vocalization, salivating, or escape attempts during recent sessions
- [ ] Puppy entered the crate calmly for this stage's session, not reluctantly
- [ ] A recent potty opportunity happened before this session started

If you can check all four, you're reasonably ready to try the next stage.
