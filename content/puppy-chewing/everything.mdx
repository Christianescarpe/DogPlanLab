<AnswerFirstSummary>
  "Chewing everything" usually isn't one problem — it's normal exploration, teething discomfort, insufficient physical/mental stimulation, or excitement/frustration, each of which responds better to a slightly different approach. What changes the answer: which of these is actually driving it for your specific puppy, which is worth a few days of honest observation before assuming you know.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## Chewing Is Normal — the Question Is What's Driving Too Much of It

All puppies chew as they explore the world, and virtually all chew more during teething, likely for gum relief.<sup>1</sup> That's not the problem to solve. The problem is chewing that's destructive, frequent, or targeting things you need to protect — and that usually comes down to one or more of a few distinct causes.

## The Real Causes

- **Normal exploration.** Puppies investigate the world with their mouths — this alone accounts for a lot of "puppy chewing" and gradually decreases with maturity and training.
- **Teething discomfort.** Especially pronounced from around 12 weeks through 6 months (see [Puppy Teething](/puppy-teething/)).
- **Insufficient stimulation.** A puppy without enough physical exercise or mental engagement (food puzzles, training, novel experiences) often chews more — chewing becomes the outlet for unmet needs.<sup>2</sup>
- **Frustration or excitement.** Chewing at a leash, a gate, or an object near something exciting the puppy can't reach (another dog, a squirrel) is a distinct pattern from bored or exploratory chewing.<sup>2</sup>
- **Chewing specifically during your absence.** If chewing is notably worse when you're not home, that's worth distinguishing from general chewing — it may connect to [Puppy Home Alone](/puppy-home-alone/) rather than being a chewing-specific issue at all.<sup>3</sup>

## A Short Way to Tell What's Driving It

Keep a quick log for a few days: when does chewing happen (bored downtime? right when you leave? during a specific type of excitement?), and what's being chewed (anything reachable, vs. specific high-value items). A pattern usually emerges fast.

## What Actually Helps

1. **Manage the environment first.** Puppy-proof reachable areas — this isn't giving up on training, it's preventing rehearsal of the wrong behavior while training is still in progress.
2. **Provide real outlets**, not just a toy sitting in a corner: food puzzle toys, appropriate chew items, and enough physical/mental engagement across the day.<sup>2</sup>
3. **Redirect and reward**, not punish. Catch your puppy chewing something inappropriate, calmly redirect to an appropriate item, and reward the switch — this teaches what to do, not just what not to do.
4. **Supervise or confine** when you can't watch directly, consistent with the same principle used in potty training.
5. **Reassess if it's specifically an alone-time pattern** — see [Puppy Home Alone](/puppy-home-alone/) rather than treating it purely as a chewing problem if that's the pattern your log reveals.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming all chewing is teething and missing a stimulation gap.
- Punishing chewing after the fact — puppies don't connect a delayed correction to the act, and it doesn't reliably reduce the behavior.
- Removing all opportunity to chew rather than redirecting to appropriate outlets — chewing is a real need, not a habit to eliminate entirely.
- Not distinguishing "chews everything, all the time" from "chews specifically when left alone," which point toward different fixes.

## Decision Table

| Pattern | Likely Driver | What Helps Most |
|---|---|---|
| Chews anything reachable, fairly constant | Exploration + insufficient outlets | More appropriate chew access, more stimulation |
| Worse around 12 weeks–6 months, gums seem involved | Teething | See [Puppy Teething](/puppy-teething/) |
| Chews at gates/leashes near something exciting | Frustration | Redirect before/during the trigger, not after |
| Notably worse specifically when you're not home | Possibly alone-time related | See [Puppy Home Alone](/puppy-home-alone/) |

## What to Do Today

1. Log chewing incidents for a few days before assuming a single cause.
2. Do one puppy-proofing pass on the most-targeted area.
3. Make sure appropriate chew items are actually within easy reach, not just available somewhere in the house.

## When to Call a Professional

Chewing that seems tied to genuine distress (not just excitement or boredom) — especially if paired with signs suggesting separation-related distress — is worth a veterinarian or certified behaviorist conversation rather than continued at-home troubleshooting alone.
