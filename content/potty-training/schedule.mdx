<AnswerFirstSummary>
  The most reliable "schedule" isn't a fixed clock — it's a small set of trigger moments (waking, eating, playing, coming out of confinement) that you take your puppy out for every time, refined by what you actually observe. How consistently you can supervise, and your specific puppy's individual pattern, is why this page is about building your own schedule, not applying someone else's.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## The Trigger-Time Method

Take your puppy outside at these moments, every time, without waiting for a signal:

- Immediately after waking (including after any nap, not just overnight sleep)
- Within about 10–15 minutes after eating
- After active play or excitement
- Right after coming out of a crate or confined space
- Roughly every 1–2 hours in between, for a very young puppy

This is a starting structure — the "Building Your Own Schedule" section below is where you adjust it to your actual puppy.

## Sample Starting Schedule (Adjust From Here)

| Time | Trigger | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Wake-up | Waking | Straight outside |
| ~15 min after breakfast | Eating | Outside |
| Mid-morning | ~1–2 hrs since last break | Outside |
| After morning play/training | Activity | Outside |
| ~15 min after lunch (if applicable) | Eating | Outside |
| Afternoon | ~1–2 hrs since last break | Outside |
| After afternoon play | Activity | Outside |
| ~15 min after dinner | Eating | Outside |
| Evening | ~1–2 hrs since last break | Outside |
| Before bed | Wind-down | Final outside trip |

## Building Your Own Schedule

1. **Start with the trigger-time list above**, applied to your actual wake time, meal times, and typical activity windows — not a generic clock.
2. **Log what happens** for a few days: which trigger moments reliably lead to elimination, and which are less predictable for your puppy specifically.
3. **Adjust the gaps.** If your puppy is consistently fine well past the 1–2 hour mark between triggers, you can space those "in-between" breaks out further. If accidents are happening before a scheduled break, tighten the gap.
4. **Watch for signals**, not just the clock — circling, sniffing intently, sudden stillness, or heading toward the door are cues your puppy may need a break sooner than scheduled.

## How the Schedule Changes as Your Puppy Gets More Reliable

As accidents become less frequent and your puppy shows more consistent signaling, you can gradually stretch the "in-between" gaps and rely more on watching for signals than on a fixed trigger list. This isn't a fixed timeline — it's based on your puppy's actual demonstrated reliability, not their age alone. (See [Puppy Schedule by Age](/puppy-schedule/by-age/) for how elimination-planning examples shift with age generally — but your puppy's actual demonstrated pattern should guide the schedule more than an age bracket does.)

## What to Do Today

1. Write down your actual wake time, meal times, and typical activity windows.
2. Build your trigger-time list using the structure above.
3. Start logging outcomes for a few days before making any adjustments.

## Common Mistakes

- Building a schedule around a generic clock instead of your puppy's actual wake/meal/activity times.
- Loosening the schedule based on age alone, before your puppy has actually demonstrated reliability.
- Abandoning the trigger-time structure the moment things start going well, rather than gradually easing off it.

## When to Call Your Veterinarian

If accidents remain frequent despite a consistently applied trigger-time schedule for a week or more, or if you notice straining, very frequent small-amount urination, or blood in urine, treat this as a reason to check with your vet rather than continuing to adjust the schedule.
