## What Zoomies Actually Are

Sudden explosions of running, spinning, and jumping — often with a tucked rear end — are common enough in puppies and young dogs to have their own name: frenetic random activity periods, or FRAPs.<sup>1</sup> This is a different behavior from nighttime biting (see [Puppy Biting at Night](/puppy-biting/at-night/)) — zoomies are whole-body, running-and-spinning behavior, not mouth-directed. They can happen together in the same evening for some puppies, but they aren't the same phenomenon and don't necessarily share the same explanation.

## Being Honest About the "Why"

We're not going to hand you a confident single cause, because the actual expert source here doesn't offer one: "There is no known specific cause of FRAPs in dogs," according to a veterinary behavior resident at Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center — though they're thought to be a way to release pent-up energy, or possibly to relieve stress.<sup>1</sup> Terms like "witching hour" and explanations like "overtired" or "not enough exercise" are common trainer/owner observations, not settled science — worth trying as practical approaches, but not something to state as proven fact.

## What Helps, Practically

- **A safe space to do it.** If your puppy is in a safe, obstacle-free area, letting a brief zoomie session run its course is generally fine rather than something to interrupt.
- **A calmer wind-down routine**, if evening zoomies are consistently disruptive — worth trying, given how commonly trainers report a connection to daily activity and rest patterns, even without proven causation.
- **Don't over-exercise a very young puppy to try to prevent this.** Excessive forced exercise isn't an appropriate fix for a normal behavior, especially in a puppy whose joints and growth plates are still developing — appropriate play and rest matter more than more exercise.
- **Redirect if the running risks a collision** with people, furniture, or stairs — guide toward open space rather than trying to stop the behavior outright.

## When to Pay Closer Attention

Zoomies are generally normal, happy behavior. If they become excessive — notably more frequent, more intense, or seem paired with signs like anxiety or overstimulation rather than the usual burst-then-done pattern — that's worth a closer look rather than assuming it's the same ordinary zoomies.<sup>2</sup>

## Quick Reference

| Pattern | Likely | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Brief (a minute or two), happens occasionally, puppy seems happy/playful | Normal FRAPs | Let it run its course in a safe space |
| Happens very frequently, seems tied to specific triggers, more intense than typical | Possibly worth a closer look | Note the pattern; consider whether anxiety/overstimulation fits better than ordinary zoomies |
| Paired with signs of distress rather than play | Different from ordinary zoomies | Discuss with your vet or a certified behaviorist |

## What to Do Today

1. Clear a safe space rather than trying to stop a normal zoomie session.
2. If evenings are consistently chaotic, try an earlier, calmer wind-down for a few nights and see if it changes anything.

## Common Mistakes

- Stating "overtired" or "not enough exercise" as the proven cause.
- Adding more vigorous exercise specifically to try to prevent zoomies — not appropriate for a young, still-developing puppy.
- Trying to physically stop a normal, safe zoomie session.

## When to Call a Professional

Zoomies that are unusually frequent, intense, or seem to be replacing normal play with something that looks more like distress — a veterinarian or certified behaviorist can help distinguish normal FRAPs from something else going on.
