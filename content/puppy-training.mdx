<AnswerFirstSummary>
  You can start training your puppy the day you bring them home — there's no reason to wait until a certain age — and the training method that works best, according to veterinary behavior science, is reward-based: rewarding what you want, not punishing what you don't.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## You Can Start Today

There's a persistent myth that puppies shouldn't start "real" training until 6 months old. That's outdated. Learning starts immediately, and short, frequent sessions from day one build the foundation everything else sits on — you're not waiting for a training-readiness switch to flip.

## The Method: Reward-Based, No Exceptions

The current, evidence-based standard from veterinary behavior organizations is clear: reward-based training methods should be used for all dog training, including working through behavior problems — with no role for aversive methods (force, pain, intimidation) at any stage, even for difficult behaviors.<sup>1</sup> That means: no yelling, no leash corrections, no prong/choke/e-collars, no alpha rolls, no physical corrections. Reward what you want to see more of; remove the reward for what you don't.

## Keep Sessions Short

A young puppy's attention span is short. A handful of 2–5 minute sessions spread across the day beats one long session — and ending on a success, not a struggle, matters more than session length.

## What to Teach, and in What Order

See [First Things to Teach a Puppy](/puppy-training/first-things-to-teach/) for the full curriculum order. In brief: attention/name recognition first, then foundational skills (sit, come), then safety skills (leave it, drop it), then walking on leash, then more advanced impulse control (settle, ignoring distractions).

## Getting Started Today

- [ ] Pick 2–3 short training moments today (not one long session)
- [ ] Have appropriate rewards on hand before you start (see [Rewards & Treats](/puppy-training/rewards/))
- [ ] Choose one skill to start with — Name & Attention is a strong first choice
- [ ] Commit to reward-based methods only — no corrections, no aversive tools

## What to Do Today

1. Do one 2–3 minute name/attention session.
2. Decide on your reward type.
3. Read [First Things to Teach a Puppy](/puppy-training/first-things-to-teach/) to plan your order.

## Common Mistakes

- Waiting for an arbitrary age to "start real training."
- Long sessions that end in frustration.
- Mixing reward-based training with corrections "just for the stubborn moments" — consistency matters, and mixing methods undermines both.

## When Professional Help May Be Appropriate

If your puppy shows fear, aggression, or a level of difficulty that goes well beyond typical puppy distraction/stubbornness, a certified trainer or veterinary behaviorist is the right next step rather than pushing harder with the same approach.
