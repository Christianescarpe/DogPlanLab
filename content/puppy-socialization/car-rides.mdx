  Build positive associations gradually — short, calm rides that end somewhere good, not just trips to the vet — and know that vomiting or distress in the car can be motion sickness (a physical, inner-ear issue common in puppies that's usually outgrown by about a year), anxiety (a learned association), or both at once.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## Two Different Problems That Can Look the Same

Motion sickness in puppies is common and is thought to relate to the balance-related parts of the inner ear not being fully developed yet — most puppies outgrow it by around a year old.<sup>1</sup> Anxiety is a learned response, often building because a puppy's only car trips are to somewhere stressful (most commonly the vet), so the car itself becomes associated with something unpleasant.<sup>1</sup><sup>2</sup> Both can cause vomiting, drooling, and distress, and a puppy can have both at once — which is why persistent symptoms are worth a vet conversation rather than a guess.<sup>2</sup>

## Building Positive Associations From the Start

1. Make some car trips go somewhere fun (a park, a friend's house) — not exclusively the vet.
2. Keep early rides short.
3. Pair the car with something positive: a favorite toy, calm praise, treats given calmly once in the car.
4. Build up gradually — sitting in a parked car first, then a short trip around the block, before longer drives.

## If Vomiting or Distress Is Happening

This is where familiarization alone isn't the fix — more rides won't resolve genuine motion sickness, and a persistently anxious puppy needs behavior-focused help, not just repetition.<sup>2</sup> Talk to your veterinarian: they can help distinguish motion sickness from anxiety and discuss options, which may include anti-nausea medication for true motion sickness or a structured desensitization plan (potentially alongside your vet) for anxiety.<sup>2</sup>

## What to Do Today

1. Schedule at least one car trip this week that doesn't end at the vet.
2. Start with the engine off, puppy in the car, treats and calm praise, then release — building up gradually over several short sessions before an actual drive.
3. Keep first real drives brief.
4. Track whether distress happens every time (more consistent with motion sickness) or seems tied to specific triggers/destinations (more consistent with anxiety) — useful information for your vet conversation.

If your puppy seems fine with a stationary car but distressed only once moving, that leans toward motion sickness; if they're anxious even before the car starts moving, that leans toward anxiety — either way, this is useful to mention to your vet rather than something to self-diagnose confidently.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming more car rides alone will fix vomiting — it won't fix true motion sickness, and it can worsen anxiety if the rides keep ending badly.
- Only ever driving your puppy to the vet.
- Ignoring persistent vomiting as "just something puppies do."

## When to Call Your Veterinarian

Persistent vomiting, drooling, or distress during car rides — talk to your veterinarian rather than continuing to guess; they can help identify whether it's motion sickness, anxiety, or both, and discuss appropriate next steps.<sup>2</sup>
