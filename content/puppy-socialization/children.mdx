  Positive, well-supervised exposure to children is an important part of socialization — but supervision is not optional. Both the American Veterinary Medical Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics are explicit: children should never be left alone with a dog, even a familiar family pet, regardless of how well-behaved either one is.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## Why "Never Leave Them Alone" Isn't an Exaggeration

This isn't overcautious advice — it's the direct, explicit position of both the AVMA and the American Academy of Pediatrics, stated without qualification: never leave children unsupervised with dogs, even familiar, well-trained family pets, because they can react unpredictably in certain situations.<sup>1</sup><sup>2</sup> More than half of all dog-related injuries are to children, and for children under four, those injuries are disproportionately to the head and neck.<sup>1</sup> This isn't about your puppy being dangerous — it's about the reality that any dog, in any specific situation, can react in a way no one predicted.

## What Positive, Supervised Socialization Looks Like

- Calm, brief, adult-supervised interactions rather than long unstructured playtime.
- Teach children to approach calmly, ask before petting, and let the puppy come to them rather than reaching for or grabbing at the puppy.
- Respect the puppy's space during eating, sleeping, or chewing — children should be taught not to disturb a dog in these moments.<sup>1</sup>
- Reward calm behavior on both sides — your puppy for calm engagement, your child for gentle, calm interaction.

## The Responsibility Isn't the Child's

Young children cannot reliably read a dog's body language or manage a puppy's behavior — that responsibility stays with the supervising adult. Teaching a child rules (ask first, be gentle, don't disturb a resting dog) is valuable, but it doesn't substitute for active adult supervision every time.<sup>1</sup>

## What to Do Today

1. Keep early interactions short, calm, and directly supervised — not "in the same room" supervision, but actively watching.
2. Teach children the basics: ask before petting, approach calmly, don't disturb a sleeping/eating dog.
3. Watch your puppy's body language throughout — stiffness, moving away, or a fixed stare are signs to end the interaction, not push through it.
4. Keep your puppy healthy — pain and illness increase the likelihood of a defensive reaction, so routine veterinary care is part of bite prevention, not a separate topic.<sup>1</sup>

If your puppy seems consistently overstimulated or avoidant specifically around children, shorten sessions and increase distance rather than assuming it will resolve with more exposure.

## Common Mistakes

- "Supervision" that means being in the same room but not actively watching.
- Letting a child ride, sit on, or hug a puppy tightly.
- Assuming a generally good-natured puppy doesn't need the same supervision as a more nervous one.

## When to Call Your Veterinarian

Any growling, snapping, or stiffening directed at a child — even once — warrants an immediate management change (separate the two, supervise more closely) and a conversation with your veterinarian or a certified trainer/behaviorist, not a wait-and-see approach.
