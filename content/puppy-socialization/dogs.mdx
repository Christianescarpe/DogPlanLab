<AnswerFirstSummary>
  Dog-to-dog socialization does not require the dog park, meeting every dog, or free-for-all play — controlled settings (vaccine-verified puppy classes, supervised one-on-one playdates with known healthy dogs, or simply calm proximity to other leashed dogs) build the same social skills with far less risk.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## Skip the Dog Park, at Least for Now

Dog parks are hard to control: you don't know the health, vaccination status, or temperament of the dogs there, and a single bad experience — being bullied, overwhelmed, or scared by an out-of-control interaction — can teach a puppy that unfamiliar dogs are frightening.<sup>1</sup> A reasonable general guideline from professional trainers is to avoid dog parks until at least 6 months old, and even then to go in with clear judgment about whether it's actually a controlled environment that day.<sup>2</sup>

## What Controlled Exposure Looks Like

- **Vaccine-verified puppy classes** with a structured format — often a better source of genuine social learning than an unstructured play free-for-all.
- **One-on-one playdates** with a known, healthy, vaccinated, well-tempered adult dog or puppy — easier to supervise and interrupt than a group setting.
- **Neutral proximity**: walking near other leashed dogs without necessarily interacting — this teaches a puppy that other dogs aren't automatically exciting or threatening, which is its own valuable skill, distinct from active play.

## Supervising Play, When It Happens

Watch body language actively, and interrupt if play looks one-sided, if one dog is always pinned or chased, or if it escalates toward stiff, fast, or high-intensity interaction.<sup>3</sup> Remove toys from group settings to avoid guarding conflicts. If your puppy seems overwhelmed, bullied, or scared, remove them — don't wait to see if it resolves on its own.<sup>3</sup>

## What to Do Today

1. Choose one controlled setting this week — a vaccine-verified class or a playdate with a known dog — rather than a dog park.
2. Watch body language throughout any interaction, not just at the start.
3. Interrupt one-sided or escalating play before it becomes a problem.
4. Practice neutral proximity (walking near other dogs without interacting) as its own valuable skill, not just a stepping stone to play.

If your puppy seems to want to interact with every dog they see and struggles to walk calmly past one, that's a training/impulse-control opportunity (see [Training Around Distractions](/puppy-training/distracted/)), not necessarily a sign they need more dog-to-dog play time.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating the dog park as a socialization requirement.
- Assuming all play is good play regardless of how it looks.
- Not removing a puppy from an interaction that's becoming one-sided or overwhelming.
- Skipping vaccination verification for the sake of convenience.

## When Professional Help May Be Appropriate

If your puppy shows fear, avoidance, or defensive behavior around other dogs — not just disinterest — see [Puppy Fear: Scared of Dogs](/puppy-fear/scared-of-dogs/). If a specific incident (an attack, a scary encounter) has already happened, see [Bad Experience](/puppy-socialization/bad-experience/).
