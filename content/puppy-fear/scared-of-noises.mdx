## The Method: Desensitization and Counterconditioning

The standard, evidence-based approach for an established sound fear is systematic desensitization combined with counterconditioning: your puppy is first conditioned to relax on cue (see [Teaching Calm & Settle](/puppy-training/calm-settle/)) in the absence of the trigger, then a very low-level version of the sound is introduced while they stay relaxed, gradually increasing intensity only as comfort holds.<sup>1</sup> This is different from simply exposing a puppy to a sound repeatedly and hoping they "get used to it" — that approach, called flooding, risks making fear significantly worse.<sup>1</sup>

## Why This Needs Real Care

Getting the exposure level wrong — too loud, too fast — can increase fear and anxiety rather than reduce it.<sup>1</sup> For anything beyond mild, occasional startle, this should be done under the direction of a qualified trainer or veterinary behaviorist who can help judge the right starting intensity and pace.<sup>1</sup>

## A Practical Starting Point for Mild Fear

1. Make sure your puppy can relax on cue in a calm environment first (see [Teaching Calm & Settle](/puppy-training/calm-settle/)).
2. Introduce a recording of the sound at a volume low enough that your puppy shows no fear response at all.
3. Pair that low-level sound with something genuinely positive.
4. Only increase volume once your puppy is consistently comfortable at the current level — this can take many sessions.
5. If at any point your puppy shows fear, you've moved too fast — go back to the last comfortable level.

## An Important Caution

Confining a fearful puppy during exposure to a real, uncontrolled version of the sound (an actual thunderstorm, real fireworks) is not the same as a controlled desensitization session, and some puppies can panic and injure themselves if confined during genuine distress — don't attempt to "practice" during a real event.<sup>2</sup>

## Common Mistakes

- Flooding — exposing at full intensity and waiting it out.
- Confining your puppy during an actual, uncontrolled loud event as if it were a training session.
- Increasing volume before your puppy is genuinely comfortable at the current level.
- Attempting a moderate-to-severe case entirely without professional guidance.

If fear returns or worsens partway through a desensitization plan, that's a sign to slow down or get professional input, not to push through faster.

## When Professional Help May Be Appropriate

Moderate to severe fear of any sound should involve a qualified trainer or veterinary behaviorist from the start, not just if home efforts fail — the guidance is explicit that this level of case benefits from professional pacing.<sup>1</sup> Any signs of panic, self-injury attempts, or extreme distress during a loud event also warrant a veterinary conversation.
