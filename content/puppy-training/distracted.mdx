<AnswerFirstSummary>
  A puppy that's reliable at home but ignores you outside isn't being defiant — dogs don't automatically generalize a learned behavior to new, more distracting environments. You have to deliberately re-teach reliability at each new level of distraction, using better rewards than the environment is offering.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## The Real Reason: You're Competing With the Environment

Your puppy repeats what's rewarding. If sniffing the ground is more interesting than what you're offering, that's what wins — not because your puppy doesn't know the command, but because the reward math doesn't favor you yet.<sup>1</sup>

## Building Reliability Through Distraction Levels

1. Confirm the behavior is genuinely solid in a quiet room first — not just "usually works," actually solid.
2. Add one mild distraction at a time (a person in the room, a toy nearby) — not a full jump to the backyard or a busy sidewalk.
3. Raise the reward value to match the distraction level — a higher-value treat for a harder environment.
4. Practice at each new distraction level until reliable before moving to the next.
5. When you do add a genuinely distracting environment (outside, around other dogs), start with the easiest version of that environment, not the hardest.

## "Watch Me" as a Reset Tool

A dedicated "watch me" cue — separate from your puppy's name — gives you a tool to pull focus back from a distraction mid-moment, which is different from (and complements) generalizing a specific command like sit or come.

## Distraction-Level Ladder

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| 1 | Quiet room, no distractions |
| 2 | Quiet room, one mild distraction (person present) |
| 3 | Backyard or quiet outdoor space |
| 4 | Quiet street, minimal traffic |
| 5 | Busier area, other dogs/people passing |

## What to Do Today

Test a "solid" command at the next distraction level up from where you've been practicing — if it falls apart, that's useful information, not a training failure; go back one level and rebuild there.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming a puppy who's reliable at home "knows" the command everywhere.
- Jumping too many distraction levels at once.
- Using the same low-value reward regardless of how distracting the environment is.
- Getting frustrated at a puppy who "should know this by now" rather than adjusting the training level.

## When Professional Help May Be Appropriate

If your puppy shows fear or extreme avoidance in more distracting environments (not just excitement/distraction), a certified trainer can help distinguish a training-level issue from a fear-based one that needs a different approach.
