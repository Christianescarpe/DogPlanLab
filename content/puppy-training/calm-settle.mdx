<AnswerFirstSummary>
  "Settle" is a trainable skill — teaching your puppy to lie calmly on a mat or bed, even with distraction around — distinct from your puppy's biological sleep needs (see [Puppy Sleep](/puppy-sleep/) for that). It's built gradually, by rewarding your puppy for choosing to relax, not by commanding relaxation directly.
</AnswerFirstSummary>

## This Is a Skill, Not Sleep

Settle training teaches an "off switch" your puppy can use during busy, exciting, or overstimulating moments — greetings, mealtimes with guests, a vet-office wait, a cafe patio — without needing to actually be tired or ready for bed. It's a different topic from biological sleep needs, and different again from evening zoomies or nighttime crying, though building this skill can genuinely help make evenings calmer over time (see [Puppy Sleep](/puppy-sleep/), [Puppy Zoomies at Night](/puppy-zoomies/at-night/)).

## Before You Start

Make sure your puppy's basic needs — exercise, mental stimulation, a reasonably predictable daily routine — are being met first. It's not realistic to expect a puppy to settle on cue if they're genuinely under-exercised or under-stimulated generally.<sup>1</sup>

## How to Teach "Go to Mat"

1. Toss a treat onto a mat or bed, saying your cue ("place," "settle," "go to bed" — pick one).
2. As your puppy steps onto the mat, mark and reward.
3. Once your puppy goes to the mat reliably, start rewarding calmer postures specifically — lying down, then a relaxed hip-shifted lie-down — rather than just "on the mat."
4. Gradually increase the time between rewards as your puppy settles more, so you're reinforcing sustained calm, not just the initial arrival.<sup>2</sup>
5. Add mild distraction once your puppy is reliably settling in a quiet setting, then gradually build toward more real-world scenarios.

## If Progress Stalls

If your puppy isn't making steady progress despite consistent practice, that's worth a closer look — there may be a physical or emotional reason your puppy isn't settling as expected, and a veterinarian or veterinary behaviorist is a reasonable next step rather than continuing to push the same approach.<sup>2</sup>

## Progression Checklist

- [ ] Reliably goes to mat on cue
- [ ] Reliably lies down on the mat
- [ ] Rewarded specifically for relaxed postures, not just lying down
- [ ] Can hold a calm settle for a short period with minimal distraction
- [ ] Building toward real-world settings (guests, outings) gradually

## What to Do Today

If you haven't started, do a few reps of tossing a treat onto a mat and rewarding your puppy for going to it. If you've started, check whether you're rewarding relaxed posture specifically, not just presence on the mat.

## Common Mistakes

- Expecting settle training to work as a substitute for adequate exercise/stimulation.
- Rushing to add distraction before the behavior is solid in a quiet setting.
- Only rewarding arrival at the mat, not sustained calm.
- Treating a stalled-progress puppy as simply "not trying" rather than considering a physical or emotional cause.

## When Professional Help May Be Appropriate

Persistent difficulty settling despite consistent, correctly paced practice and adequate exercise/stimulation is worth a veterinary or veterinary behaviorist visit — it may reflect something beyond a training gap.<sup>2</sup>
